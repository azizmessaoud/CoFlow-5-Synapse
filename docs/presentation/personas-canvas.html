CoFlow-5 — what we have reached (simple words)

Say this to the supervisor: we are in the Empathize stage of Design Thinking. We know who we design for. We have not programmed the traffic lights in SUMO yet.

  • 8 people (sheets below — same canvas as the course).
  • Their day: where it hurts (waiting, late buses, blocked ambulance, pollution by the school).
  • Evidence: papers and guides (e.g. a wait feels about twice as long; irregular buses hurt more than slightly slower buses).
  • Ideas: one boss of the lights; other agents only ask (pedestrian, bus, ambulance, pollution, incidents). A talking AI never presses a light.
  • Numbers (40 seconds, −25%…) = targets to test, not results we already measured.

Architecture for the professor (agents, messages, build order): print the next pages, or see system-design-for-professor.md.

How many agents? (five + one explainer)

CoFlow-5 = 5 agents. Only A1 may change a light. A2–A5 only ask. The LLM is not a sixth traffic agent.

AgentJobWrites lights?
A1 FlowBoss of the lights — picks the phase and who winsYes — only A1
A2 EmergencyAmbulance corridor — asks to clear the queue aheadNo
A3 MultimodalSlow walkers + late buses (not every bus)No
A4 SituationForecast + “this does not match the forecast”No
A5 SustainabilityCare about emission hot-spots when delay allowsNo
Synapse (LLM)Explains the decision log in wordsNever

Three rules: (1) Safety first — no fighting greens; a person already crossing finishes. (2) The boss is boss. (3) Backup: if messages die, A1 still runs; if the learned policy dies → Max-Pressure → actuated → fixed-time.

How they communicate

Noticeboard (pub/sub): agents post and read. Topics: state/ (queues, data age), forecast/ (A4), alerts/ (incidents, stale data), eco/ (A5 weights). Every message has an expiry. Silent A4 ≠ frozen lights.

Formal ask (one round, 5–10 s): A2/A3 send a request → A1 accept or reject + a reason code Rosa can read. Priority above the auction: safety / person on crossing → ambulance → pedestrian deadline → late bus → general flow → eco.

Tech to start: asyncio pub/sub in one process. Redis only if we split machines. Kafka is too heavy for this prototype.

If the board is empty or old, A1 still acts. We will later drop messages on purpose to prove that.

Best way to build (please verify this plan)

  1. Spine: pin SUMO, 4×4 grid, fixed-time + actuated baselines. No “AI” required yet.
  2. Tier 0 (MVP): A1 vs those baselines and Max-Pressure. Safety mask. Honest stats (no best-episode tables). This alone can satisfy the brief.
  3. Tier 1: bus + A2, A3, A5. Turn agents off (ablation) to show each one earns its keep.
  4. Tier 2: A4 + incident flags + communication-loss tests. Bigger map only after the grid is solid.
  5. LLM (optional, parallel): explain the log. If time is short, cut the LLM, not the evaluation.

Reject us if we: let the LLM set a phase; report the best training episode as the result; turn simulated ambulance seconds into “lives saved”; call HBEFA “air quality”; add A2–A5 before A1 vs Max-Pressure runs.

Name: Amara

Frustrations and fears:

She is afraid she will still be on the crossing when cars get a green. Waiting makes her tired. If the green is too short, she feels unsafe. She fears the light is designed only for young, fast walkers.

Personality:

Careful, knows her neighbourhood, slow but determined. She does not want to “break traffic”. She just wants to cross safely.

Quote:

“I need enough time to cross, even when the street is busy.”

Needs and expectations:

A button or detector she understands. A clear sign that her request was received. Enough green time (she walks at about 0.8 m/s with a cane). Not waiting too long (after about 30 seconds many people cross on red — that is a design problem, not her fault).

Technologies used:

Push-button at the crossing. No smartphone required. Simple sound or light feedback. No app.

Age: 74
Profession: Retired
Situation: Walks with a cane; shops + bus twice a day
City: Tunis (busy centre street)

Bio:

Amara crosses the main street twice a day for shops and the bus. She is not “anti-car”. She needs a system built for a slow walker, not for the average person.

What she does not want:

A green that is too short. Having to download an app. Cutting the pedestrian green for an ambulance while she is already on the road. Presenting “40 seconds max” as a proven result — it is a target to test.

Name: David

Frustrations and fears:

He can accept a longer trip. He cannot accept not knowing if it will take 20 or 50 minutes. Small delays stack up and he misses delivery windows. He fears “average speed” that hides the worst days.

Personality:

Organised, driven by the schedule, practical. He plans his round; uncertainty breaks his job.

Quote:

“I can plan around a longer trip if I know how long it will actually take.”

Needs and expectations:

More stable travel times (especially the bad days: the slow tail, not only the average). Fewer useless stops at lights. The system should see jams and incidents, not only one junction on its own.

Technologies used:

GPS / Waze-style app, work phone, delivery-round app.

Age: 41
Profession: Delivery driver
Situation: 60+ timed stops per day
City: Tunis (main roads and side streets)

Bio:

David delivers all day. A “smart” junction that improves the average but makes the worst trips worse still fails him. In our tests, an unfinished trip must count as a failure, not hidden time.

What he does not want:

Boasting about a faster average while his worst rounds get worse. Ignoring unfinished deliveries in the statistics.

Name: Chidi

Frustrations and fears:

He waits a long time, then three buses arrive together. He misses his connection. He fears “bus priority” only means “faster buses” when he needs regular buses.

Personality:

Daily rider, no fixed timetable: regularity is the service. Impatient when waiting is unpredictable.

Quote:

“Buses should arrive regularly — not three at once after a long wait.”

Needs and expectations:

Priority mainly for the late bus / the one after a big gap, not for every bus. Less bunching. Waiting at the stop matters more than a few minutes on the bus (research often values wait about 2× in-vehicle time).

Technologies used:

Bus GPS / timetable apps if they exist. Otherwise: watching the street. No work tools.

Age: 27
Profession: Employee, frequent bus user
Situation: High-frequency line, no real timetable
City: Tunis (centre bus corridor)

Bio:

Chidi takes the bus every day. On a frequent line you do not “catch a timetable”: you wait for the next one. If gaps explode, his day breaks.

What he does not want:

Bus priority that cuts pedestrians (Amara) short. Measuring only bus travel time and not passenger waiting. A made-up “X% of riders quit the bus” number (we do not have that figure).

Name: Rosa

Frustrations and fears:

She cannot see why a bus got (or did not get) priority. With no history she cannot explain an incident. She fears a “black box” she will be asked to defend.

Personality:

Responsible, calm under pressure, needs proof. She wants to command, not only watch a pretty screen.

Quote:

“Show me why a bus got — or was denied — priority, and let me act before the service breaks.”

Needs and expectations:

A simple screen: positions, gaps, priority yes/no. A reason code (“pedestrian still on the crossing”, “queue blocked behind”). Ability to request priority without breaking light safety. A time-stamped log.

Technologies used:

Depot / bus-location tools, radio, spreadsheet, maybe an operations dashboard.

Age: 52
Profession: Bus depot controller
Situation: Handles line disruptions
City: Tunis (operations desk)

Bio:

Rosa watches bus service. Real systems (e.g. SCATS) already sell manual takeover and audit trails: that is the job, not a luxury.

What she does not want:

Magic “5-minute warnings” sold as already proven. An AI that decides for her with no readable reason. A fake “X% of engineers distrust AI” statistic (no solid survey).

Name: Marcus

Frustrations and fears:

A green light is useless if the queue has nowhere to go. He fears jumping from junction to junction and leaving chaos behind (cars, buses, pedestrians). He does not want a simulation turned into “lives saved”.

Personality:

Urgent, precise, fair: he knows other people exist. The job is to arrive and let the network recover.

Quote:

“Get us through safely, and make sure traffic recovers after we pass.”

Needs and expectations:

The system knows an ambulance is coming (trusted request). Clear a bit of the queue ahead, not only the light under the bumper. Pedestrians already on the crossing finish. After the pass: a gentle return, not a shock.

Technologies used:

Radio, dispatch tablet, GPS, lights and siren. No public app.

Age: 34
Profession: Ambulance crew (paramedic)
Situation: Urban calls in peak hours
City: Tunis (central hospital → dense streets)

Bio:

Minutes matter in medical literature (survival tends to fall when advanced care arrives later). But our simulation must never say “we saved X lives”. We measure ambulance time and delay for everyone else.

What he does not want:

Cutting off a pedestrian already on the road. A made-up “flashover in 3–5 minutes” claim. Hiding the cost for cars and buses. A single green that only moves the jam to the next junction.

Name: Yuki

Frustrations and fears:

She is still responsible if the system does something stupid. She fears stale data that looks normal. She does not want “smart” mode with no way to take back control — but takeover must never turn on two greens that conflict.

Personality:

Careful, process-driven, accountable. She prefers a simple fallback (fixed plan) to an AI she cannot explain.

Quote:

“Automation should support my decisions — not leave me responsible for a system I cannot control.”

Needs and expectations:

See: current mode, how fresh the data is, faults. A history: what the system saw, decided, and why. If it breaks: a backup ladder (learned policy → Max-Pressure → actuated lights → fixed plan). Manual takeover under safety rules.

Technologies used:

Signal supervisor, logs, sensor status, SCATS-like tools. Maybe Python / GIS on the side. The need is the same: she must explain what happened.

Age: 47
Profession: Traffic engineer / operations
Situation: Accountable for network safety
City: Tunis (control room)

Bio:

Yuki needs to sleep at night: if a sensor dies, the system must say so and fall back to a known mode. Adaptive control can lose its benefits in heavy congestion: fallback is not optional.

What she does not want:

An AI in the 1-second control loop (too slow, too fragile). Manual takeover that creates conflicting greens. Presenting the LLM as the boss of the lights.

Name: Omar

Frustrations and fears:

Too many false alarms and he stops listening. An alert with no “why” does not help. A frozen screen can hide a failure. He fears we compare our detection to a “world average delay” that does not exist.

Personality:

Sceptical, methodical, operational. He wants to sort: real incident, ordinary jam, or dead sensor.

Quote:

“Tell me what is wrong, why you think so, and whether I can still trust the data.”

Needs and expectations:

Grouped alerts with level and clues. Mark data that is too old. Separate “nothing found” from “we do not know”. Track recovery after the blockage is cleared. Incidents are a large share of congestion (about 25–30% in FHWA-type sources) — that is why we care, not a magic threshold.

Technologies used:

Radio, cameras / video wall, phone, incident tools. Not necessarily visible machine learning.

Age: 55
Profession: Duty officer (incidents)
Situation: Coordinates the operational response
City: Tunis (incident desk)

Bio:

Omar lives with queues that might be a crash, demand, or a broken sensor. Our proposed method: compare reality to a simple forecast, then alert — thresholds come after a small pilot, not from a number nobody published.

What he does not want:

One fake “average detection delay” for the whole world. Alerts with no confidence. Pretending an anomaly already tells us the cause.
